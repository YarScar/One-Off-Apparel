# Literal/Concrete Writing Style Guide

This is the single most important constraint on this skill's output. Launchpad's team asked for this
explicitly because grant reports are a trust document — funders make future funding decisions based on
what's written here, and a number or claim that turns out to be inflated damages that relationship far
more than an honest but less flattering result ever would.

## The rule

Report final outcomes and facts only. Do not spin, round favorably, or substitute a softer claim for
what the data actually shows. If the real number or outcome isn't flattering, report it accurately
anyway — that is the point of this skill, not a side effect of it.

## What "spin" looks like (avoid these patterns)

- Vague intensifiers standing in for a number: "significant improvement," "tremendous growth," "robust
  engagement." If a stat exists, use it. If it doesn't, say what you actually know instead of reaching
  for an adjective.
- Passive framing that obscures who did what or hides a shortfall: "targets were not fully met" instead
  of "14 of the planned 20 students completed the program."
- Cherry-picking the best-looking number when multiple exist. If enrollment was 40 and completion was
  22, report both — don't lead with enrollment alone to imply a different completion story.
- Reframing a missed goal as a pivot without saying the goal was missed. It's fine to explain *why*
  performance differed from expectations (that's literally one of the three required report elements),
  but the explanation comes after stating the actual result plainly.
- Using a range or approximation when an exact figure is available in the source documents.

## What good literal writing looks like

**Weak (spin):** "The program saw strong participation and meaningful outcomes for students throughout
the grant period."

**Strong (literal):** "32 students enrolled in Cohort 3; 26 completed the full curriculum (an 81%
completion rate, up from 74% in Cohort 2). The 6 students who did not complete cited scheduling
conflicts with after-school jobs in exit surveys."

**Weak (spin):** "Despite some challenges, the organization made excellent progress toward its goals."

**Strong (literal):** "The organization completed 3 of the 4 planned workshops. The fourth was postponed
to the following quarter after the original venue partner withdrew in March; it was rescheduled and held
in June."

## Handling numbers that don't match across sources

Grant tracking data, prior reports, and source documentation sometimes disagree (Launchpad's team
flagged a real case where a reported number changed from 17 to 19 between drafts). When source
documents disagree on a figure:

1. Do not silently pick one and move on.
2. Do not average or round them together.
3. Flag it in the inconsistency section of the draft (see `inconsistency-and-citations.md`), in one line
   with both figures, their sources, and — if apparent — why they might differ (e.g., "17 reflects the
   mid-year report; 19 is the year-end tracking sheet, which may include two late enrollments"). One
   sentence is almost always enough; save a longer explanation for the rare case where the reviewer
   truly can't act on a one-liner.
4. Leave the actual number choice to the human reviewer rather than guessing which is "more correct."

**Exception — a stale summary line within the same document.** The rule above is for numbers that
conflict *across* independent sources (two different reports, two different tracking sheets). It's a
different situation when a single document contains both a structured, itemized table (e.g., an
enrollment table broken out by cohort) and a separate prose summary elsewhere in that *same* document
that states an older or rounder version of the same figure. In that case the structured, itemized data
is almost always the more current and more authoritative one — the prose line is typically just an
unrevised holdover from an earlier draft of the same doc. Use the table figure in the narrative and note
the stale prose figure informationally in the inconsistency log (so a human can see it was noticed and
already resolved), rather than raising it as an open `[NEEDS INPUT]` that blocks the draft — a reviewer
seeing their own document's internal table treated as an unresolved mystery reads as the draft not having
looked closely enough, not as appropriate caution.

## Match Launchpad's current program language

Program names and descriptions are not static — the Launchpad team actively revises how each program is
described, and the **Launchpad Programming MASTER** doc
(`https://docs.google.com/document/d/1YYEsi0PWm0oCtKAG_8jyQuP2PFEOhovnFroxkVeJ-WI/edit`) is where that
current language lives. Open it before drafting any narrative, and treat it as the authority on program
names and descriptions — more authoritative than a prior report, and more authoritative than the grant
agreement's own phrasing if the two differ, since the agreement's language was fixed at signing while the
MASTER doc is kept current. If the MASTER doc's language for a program has changed since the last report
for this funder, use the current language rather than matching the older report's wording, and don't treat
that shift as an inconsistency to flag — it's an intentional update, not a discrepancy.

## Using the Quote Bank

The **Quote Bank** spreadsheet
(`https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1Yuj3jvp0095TvmRLU61-W6ChdcFuM2DWHDwnVT0X3uc/edit`) collects
quotes from students, clients, and employers. When a narrative question calls for qualitative voice — a
lessons-learned answer, a specific participant experience, anything a plain statistic wouldn't fully
capture — check it for a quote that fits before concluding none exists. A quote pulled from the Quote Bank
still needs an inline citation like any other fact (see `inconsistency-and-citations.md`), and it still has
to actually fit what's being asked — don't force one in just because it's available. Never write a quote
that isn't drawn verbatim from the Quote Bank or another source document; an invented or paraphrased quote
attributed to a real person is exactly the kind of thing this skill exists to prevent.

## Tone

Write in plain, direct sentences. It's fine for the writing to be warm and readable — Launchpad's
mission-driven work deserves to be described clearly — but every claim of impact needs to trace back to
a fact in the source documents. If you're drafting a sentence and can't point to where the underlying
fact came from, don't include it.

## Length

Literal doesn't mean exhaustive. Being accurate about a shortfall takes one honest sentence, not three —
compare "14 of the planned 20 students completed the program; the other 6 cited scheduling conflicts" (one
sentence, fully literal) to a paragraph that states the same fact, then restates it with more hedging,
then adds a second supporting figure that wasn't asked for. The second isn't more honest, just longer.
When drafting an answer, aim to state the result, the one figure or two that support it, and — if the
question asks for it — the reason in as few sentences as the facts require. If you find yourself writing
a second paragraph to cover a single funder question, check whether some of it is restating what the
first paragraph already said, or including a figure that isn't actually needed to answer what was asked.

## Handling gaps (no source material for a question)

Some narrative questions won't have any supporting source material at all — not a conflicting number,
just nothing to go on (e.g., no document addresses leadership changes, or no doc explains what caused a
shortfall). Don't extend or guess an answer to fill the space, and don't assume a "no change" or "nothing
to report" answer just because a topic wasn't mentioned anywhere — absence of a document is not the same
as absence of the thing itself. Instead, write the question with a placeholder like `[NEEDS INPUT: what's
missing]` describing exactly what's needed, so a human can fill it in from firsthand knowledge. A visible
gap is far more useful to a reviewer than a plausible-sounding sentence with nothing behind it.
